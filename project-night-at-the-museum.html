A Night at the Museum

VR narrative experience · Narrativas en Realidad Virtual y Mixta, Universidad EAFIT · 2026

My role
Team of 6. I was the lead Unity developer and coordinated the build
Tools
Unity · Meta Quest 3 · GitHub · AI-assisted scripting
Core mechanic
A single magic paintbrush

The problem

The experience needed one interaction — a magic paintbrush — to carry the entire story: the user wakes inside a museum and must escape the night by "activating" paintings, each one a portal into the emotional world it depicts. The paintbrush had to work as a piece of narrative (a believable in-fiction object) and, at the same time, hold up as a real piece of interaction design — the player has to always know what the brush can do, when their input has registered, and what's about to happen next, without a single line of on-screen instruction once they're inside the headset.

My role

We were a team of six. My teammates wrote the narrative, and I took the lead on turning our one-page design into a working VR experience, developing it alongside one other teammate. I was responsible for bringing it to life in Unity:

  • Built the scenes, set up the assets, and organised the folders and scripts.
  • Programmed and tested every mechanic, and got imported animations working.
  • Fixed texture and lighting import problems, and adjusted the scenery and colour scheme to match our one-page design and the paintings we chose.
  • Taught the team to use GitHub, so everyone could work on the same project from their own computers.
  • Used AI to draft a development schedule with tasks for each member before our meetings, which we then refined together. I also checked in with people and assigned responsibilities as deadlines got closer.
  • Used AI to help write scripts and debug Unity problems, with very specific prompts so it did exactly what I asked and nothing more.

We ran into real problems along the way: syncing each other's changes, mechanics that broke, optimisation, and transitions between levels. We still shipped a complete experience.

Process

The world is built as four connected paintings-turned-realities: the Museum (the starting point), Dreaming Hills (an oneiric forest), the home of "the old blind guitarist" (a coastal village built from a blue-period Picasso), and The Blue Grotto (a dark reef). The brush starts as a simple activation tool — opening portals, lighting spaces, interacting with the environment — and evolves into "magic ink": first a way into new worlds, then a light source and orientation tool in the dark, and finally a creative tool for transforming the environment itself. Each world was mapped with its own emotional arc, its own map, and its own set of active mechanics, worked out as a one-page design summary before anything was built.

The core interaction sequence — point, hold, activate — was worked out as a four-panel storyboard: the player aims the brush at a painting, holds the action for a few seconds while the system registers it, and watches the painting light up as it activates. That storyboard became the spec for the feedback system: over roughly five seconds, progressive haptic vibration in the controller runs alongside visual changes — the painting beginning to glow, brushstrokes moving, particles of paint appearing — so the player never has to guess whether their input registered.

To check that the mechanic worked as interaction design and not just as part of the story, the team mapped it directly onto Norman's seven-stage Model of Action: intention (decide to use the brush on the painting), specify the action (aim, guided by signifiers — a voice giving instructions and a ghostly hand pointing), execute (hold the brush against the frame) — causing a change in the world — then perceive (the painting starts glowing), interpret (understand the portal is activating), and evaluate (the portal opens and the goal — entering the new world — is met). Walking the mechanic through every stage surfaced exactly where a signifier was still missing rather than leaving it to guesswork.

Solution

A single, legible interaction — aim, hold, feel it respond, watch it open — reused across four completely different worlds. The transition itself is treated as its own beat: the painting stops being static, its brushstrokes expand to engulf the player's field of view, and the environment changes around them before they realise they've already arrived in the new level.

Result & reflection

A playable VR experience on Meta Quest 3, with a menu, a tutorial, transitions between portals and interactive levels (the boat, the sculpture and the full brush interaction). I didn't make the final video or submit the project, and that final build was lost. What survives are the screen recordings I made during development to keep the team updated. They show the experience working step by step.

This project taught me how to turn a team's design into something that actually runs. I learnt to keep six people working on one codebase and to use AI as a precise tool rather than a shortcut. When the pressure was on, the job was keeping the whole thing together.
