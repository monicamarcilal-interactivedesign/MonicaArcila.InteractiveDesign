A Night at the Museum

VR narrative experience · Narrativas en Realidad Virtual y Mixta, Universidad EAFIT · 2026

My role
Team of 6. Lead Unity developer: the project lived on my computer, I programmed with one teammate and I coordinated the build
Tools
Unity · Meta Quest 3 · GitHub · AI-assisted scripting
Core mechanic
A single magic paintbrush
  • Context

    A VR course at Universidad EAFIT. Our team of six designed an experience set inside four famous paintings.

  • Challenge

    One interaction, a magic paintbrush, had to carry the whole story and stay clear without menus.

  • Solution

    Aim, hold, activate: about five seconds of vibration and visual feedback, reused in every world. We built it in Unity for Meta Quest 3, and I coordinated the build.

  • Result

    A playable experience of around eight minutes, with a menu, tutorial and portal transitions. Its scenes worked as designed in Unity's simulator.

  • Learning

    Test on the headset from the start, not only in the simulator.

The problem

One interaction had to carry a whole story. The player wakes in a museum they must escape by "activating" paintings, and each painting is a portal into the emotional world it shows.

The magic paintbrush had to be a believable object in the story and a clear piece of interaction design. The player should always know what the brush can do, when their input registered and what happens next. Instructions live in the world, through a narrating voice and a pointing ghost hand, rather than in menus.

My role

Lead Unity developer in a team of six. My teammates wrote the narrative. The project lived on my computer and I was the lead developer: one teammate and I turned our one-page design into a working VR experience, programming and integrating it together, and I put in most of the hours.

  • Built the scenes myself: the whole museum, the tutorial, the menu and the whole last level, setting up the assets, colours and textures. I also organised the folders and scripts.
  • Programmed every mechanic together with one teammate, and got imported animations working. I tested mostly on my own, sometimes with teammates.
  • Built the boat mechanic on my own and made it work in the other levels that need it.
  • Found all the sound assets. Fixed texture problems (I did not make any textures) and matched the lighting and the colour of each scene to the real paintings we used as references, and to what we wanted that scene to communicate.
  • Taught the team GitHub so everyone could work on the same project.
  • Used AI to draft a task schedule before our meetings, which we then refined together, and checked in with people as deadlines got close.
  • Used AI to help write scripts and debug Unity, with very specific prompts so it did exactly what I asked.

How it works

  1. Four worlds, one brush

    The Museum (start), a dreamlike forest (Dreaming Hills), a coastal village built from Picasso's blind guitarist, and a dark reef (The Blue Grotto). Each has its own emotional arc, map and mechanics. The brush grows from a tool that opens portals into "magic ink": a light in the dark, then a way to transform the world.

  2. Aim, hold, activate

    The player points the brush at a painting and holds for a few seconds. The system recognises it and starts a gradual activation. We worked this out as a four-panel storyboard first.

  3. Five seconds of feedback

    The controller vibrates more and more while the painting glows, brushstrokes move and paint particles appear, so the player never has to guess whether it registered. Then the strokes expand around the player's view and the new world appears.

  4. One master script

    In Unity, all the brush logic lives in one script. When it is grabbed the hand closes into a fist. With ink, a shader lights the tip and particles switch on. An ink counter runs down as you use ink to teleport and to "paint", so you have to look for more. We also made the painting shaders, the animations and the transitions.

Checking the brush against Norman's Model of Action

The team mapped the mechanic onto Norman's seven stages to find missing signifiers. Intention: decide to use the brush on the painting. Specify: aim, guided by a voice and a ghostly hand that points. Execute: hold the brush on the frame. Perceive: the painting glows. Interpret: the portal is activating. Evaluate: the portal opens and the goal, entering the new world, is met.

Decisions and obstacles

  • Decision

    One tool for everything

    Instead of many mechanics, one brush that changes meaning in each world. It keeps the learning small and the story consistent.

  • Obstacle

    Six people, one project

    We struggled to sync each other's changes. I taught the team GitHub and planned who did what, week by week.

  • Obstacle

    Mechanics, optimisation and transitions

    Many scripts, many hours of testing. Import problems, broken mechanics and level transitions were fixed one by one.

How the team planned the work

We worked to a dated plan from 14 April to 19 May, in three pairs: programming and integration (me and one teammate), assets and spaces, and narrative with dialogue and the graphic line. The board is in the gallery below.

Result & what I learnt

  • 6people on the team
  • 4connected worlds
  • ~8 minfor a full run
  • 5 wks14 April – 19 May

A playable VR experience for Meta Quest 3, with a menu, a tutorial, portal transitions and interactive levels (the boat, the sculpture and the full brush interaction). In Unity's simulator, the scenes worked as we designed them.

What we don't know: when my teacher tried it on a headset, the experience stopped as he entered the third level. We never found out why, because the final build wasn't kept. I didn't make the final video or submit the project, so what survives are the screen recordings I made during development.

I learnt how to turn a team's design into something that runs, how to keep six people on one codebase and how to use AI as a precise tool rather than a shortcut. And I learnt that a simulator isn't a headset: I would test on the device much earlier.
