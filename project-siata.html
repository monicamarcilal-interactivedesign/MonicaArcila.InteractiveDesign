SIATA Geoportal Ciudadanos

Lean UX redesign · Mobile web prototype · User Experience, Universidad EAFIT · 2026

My role
Team of 5. I did the user interviews and testing, built the working prototype and wrote the analytics report
Tools
Figma, Gemini Canvas (HTML prototype), Optimal Workshop, Maze, Hotjar
Timeframe
March – May 2026

The problem

SIATA is the early-warning and environmental monitoring system for Medellín's metropolitan area. It runs two websites: a technical one and one meant for ordinary citizens. Most people don't know the citizen portal exists. When they search for SIATA they land on the technical site, where they meet unexplained abbreviations, crowded screens and no guidance.

The course brief asked us to pick a public website or app and redesign it with a Lean UX process. We chose the citizen portal and asked one question: how can someone check the weather and environmental risks, such as rain or overflowing streams, without needing to understand meteorology?

My role

This was a five-person team project. I ran and recorded user interviews, shared the test links and helped build the Figma baseline prototype. I then developed the final working prototype as HTML using Gemini Canvas. In our weekly meetings I helped decide which functions to change, rewrote SIATA's technical terms in plain language and made the vector assets that explain the UI and buttons. I also wrote an individual analytics report on how people actually used the prototype.

Process

  1. 1 — Frame the assumptions

    We started with a Lean UX Canvas (Jeff Gothelf). It set out the business problem, two proto-personas (young adults aged 17–28 and adults aged 29–50) and the risky assumptions to test first. Could people understand the current terms and icons? Which alerts mattered to them? Would a widget actually be useful?

  2. 2 — Interviews (27 March 2026)

    We interviewed six students from different degrees and heard the same thing again and again. Most people only check the weather when they have a practical decision to make, like whether to take an umbrella. Most used Apple Weather or Google instead of SIATA, because SIATA felt complex and overly technical. In their own words: "It should be simple and just show me if it's going to rain."

  3. 3 — Cognitive walkthroughs, Likert, tree testing

    We ran in-person walkthroughs and a Likert survey, then a tree test of the navigation. People went straight for the most visual elements, like the cloud, the location pin and the rain layer. They hesitated over technical category names and sometimes didn't recognise cards as buttons. The information architecture was partly clear, but the labels needed to be written in everyday language.

  4. 4 — Collaborative prototyping

    We gave participants our first structure and asked them to rearrange it. They consistently asked for fewer categories, a more direct route to navigation and alerts, and current conditions on the home screen.

  5. 5 — Build and evaluate (May 2026)

    I rebuilt the prototype as a working mobile web app. It opens with a splash screen and an "Explorar" (Explore) button, then goes to a live map with switchable layers for rain, air quality and river levels. It also has two practical tools: Clima origen–destino, which checks the weather along your route, and Mejor hora para salir, which suggests the best time to leave to avoid rain or traffic. We tested it without a moderator, using Maze missions and Hotjar recordings and heatmaps.

Solution

A map-first citizen portal that answers one question quickly: is it going to rain where I'm going, and what should I do about it? Technical variables are still available as optional map layers but no longer lead the page. Labels are written in everyday Spanish. The two route-based functions turn weather data into a decision about when and how to travel.

Result & reflection

Hotjar recorded 36 sessions (half on phones, half on computers), with an average of 4:52 minutes and 15.4 pages per session, 981 clicks and no rage clicks. Clicks clustered on the Explorar button and the map controls, so people found the entry point and treated the map as the heart of the experience. "Consultar ruta" (check your route, 17 sessions) and "Mejor hora para salir" (16 sessions) were among the most-used features.

Not everything worked, and I reported the problems too. Maze showed 0% mission success. That wasn't because everyone failed: the paths were never reviewed and categorised by hand, so the tool couldn't score them. Hotjar also couldn't track parts of the prototype that were embedded as iframes. I recorded both as limitations instead of treating the numbers as final. My recommendations were clearer active and inactive states for the layer buttons, short helper text, less visual load when many layers are on, and a re-test with light moderation. For me, being honest about what the data can't show is part of doing UX research properly.
