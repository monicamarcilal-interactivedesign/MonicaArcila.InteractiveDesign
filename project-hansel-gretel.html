Hansel & Gretel: Blood and Crumbs

3D horror survival game · Intro. a la Computación Gráfica, Universidad EAFIT · 2025

My role
Team of 6. I led development and made the final build; a teammate and I programmed it, trading my computer for the parts we split
Tools
Unity
Genre
First/third-person survival horror
  • Context

    Intro to Computer Graphics at Universidad EAFIT. A team of six turned a written story into a 3D horror game in Unity.

  • Challenge

    Break the player's scepticism one clue at a time, without ever telling them outright what is happening.

  • Solution

    A four-level house built around a six-beat horror structure. The fear escalates through the environment, not through explanations.

  • Result

    A finished, playable game. Presenting it led my lecturer to invite me into his research seedbed as a developer.

  • Learning

    When a teammate falls behind, find what you can advance while you wait, and how to help.

The problem

Make a sceptic believe, without telling him. Local legend says two siblings, Hansel and Gretel, vanished in the forest near their village. The protagonist mocks the story, until a prank by his friends leaves him genuinely lost in that same forest.

The design challenge was a horror game where the player's scepticism collapses in real time, one clue at a time.

My role

I led development. In the course we practised Unity basics: NavMesh, NPCs that follow the player, particles, trigger-based interactions, Mixamo animations and basic coding. For the final project we wrote a narrative, planned it on a single page and built a 3D game from it. A teammate and I programmed it together, meeting in person and trading my computer for the parts we split, because we had not risked working with GitHub yet. The project lived on my computer and I did most of the programming. I set high expectations for my assignments, so I spent many, many hours on it.

  • Wrote most of the scripts, with my teammate writing some, and worked with several scenes and shaders.
  • Checked every mechanic and interaction against the one-page plan, and pushed to update the plan when something had to change.
  • Asked teammates for the assets, animations and interfaces we needed.
  • Debugged with my lecturer, online research or AI. I used AI only for code and mechanics.
  • Made the final build.

The plan: six beats

The written brief didn't survive, but a dense one-page design did, detailed enough to stand in for it. The goal: find a way home, or at least survive the night, evading "the witch". Five mechanics carry it (exploration, escape, collection, reading and mini-puzzles), plus jumpscares at key story beats. The house has four levels: ground floor, second floor, basement and attic.

  1. The forest

    Objects left by the protagonist's friends nudge him deeper while the mood shifts from calm to watched.

  2. The note

    Inside the house, a note says Hansel and Gretel are held somewhere below. It triggers the first scare.

  3. The basement

    Candlelight shows a room of bones the moment the flame goes out, and a now-monstrous Hansel and Gretel start the chase.

  4. The house turns

    Escaping locks the door behind him. Objects move on their own and distorted voices follow him from nearby rooms.

  5. The second floor

    Ruined children's bedrooms and a library hide a note pointing to a key in the attic.

  6. The attic

    The truth: the siblings are trapped in a repeating cycle and need someone to take their place. One way out, a window, and one open question: will he jump, or be caught?

How the story is told

Voiceover and written notes carry the explicit story. Abandoned toys, blood on the walls and unexplained shadows carry the rest. The sound follows the same "show, don't tell" logic: creaking wood, distorted voices, and sudden silence when a candle goes out.

Decisions and obstacles

  • Decision

    Escalate through the environment

    Degrading light, objects that move between visits and a door that locks behind you replace exposition. You can no longer backtrack.

  • Decision

    Save the jumpscare for the moment of doubt

    The one that matters comes when the player reads Hansel and Gretel's note in the kitchen, exactly when their scepticism should break.

  • Obstacle

    Things that wouldn't work

    When a script, a piece of logic or a transition broke, I asked: what are the basic steps to make it work, and what do I do if it still doesn't? That became my debugging routine.

  • Obstacle

    A teammate falling behind

    I worked out what was going on, how I could help, and how to keep advancing while I waited, so the deadline didn't slip.

  • Obstacle

    A bug that came and went

    The hover on the road home sometimes worked and sometimes didn't. We spent many hours on it and even asked a second teacher for help in office hours. He didn't follow our logic, but we kept going. It was finally fixed when our main teacher changed something that didn't seem related. We never learnt what had changed, so I saved and protected the project and became much more careful with every change.

Result & reflection

  • 6people on the team
  • ~6 minfor a full play
  • 4levels in the house
  • 6story beats
  • 1invitation into a research seedbed

A finished, playable Unity game. I made the final build, and the gameplay video we submitted survives. The one-page design behind it is a complete plan on its own: narrative, floor plans, the six-beat structure, mechanics, sound design and a hand-drawn storyboard.

It also led to something bigger. After I presented the game, my lecturer invited me into his research seedbed as a developer (see VR & Emotion Research).

It was one of my first big projects in Unity. I also learnt to work in a way that was less stressful and more present.
