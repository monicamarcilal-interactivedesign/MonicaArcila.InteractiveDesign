Hansel & Gretel: Blood and Crumbs

3D horror survival game · Intro. a la Computación Gráfica, Universidad EAFIT · 2025

My role
Team of about six. I led development and made the final build
Tools
Unity
Genre
First/third-person survival horror

The problem

Local legend says two siblings, Hansel and Gretel, vanished without a trace in the forest near their village. The protagonist mocks the story — until a prank by his friends leaves him genuinely lost and disoriented in that same forest. The design challenge was to build a horror game where the player's skepticism collapses in real time, one clue at a time, without ever telling them outright what's actually happening.

My role

During the course we practised the basics of Unity: NavMesh, NPCs that follow the player, particles, trigger-based interactions, Mixamo animations and basic coding. For the final project we wrote a narrative, planned it on a single page and then built a 3D game from it.

I took the lead on development, alongside one other developer. Anything to do with making it work was my job. I wrote the scripts, worked with multiple scenes and shaders, and checked every mechanic and interaction against the one-page plan. I asked teammates for the assets, animations and interfaces we needed, and pushed to update the plan when something had to change. When a script, a piece of logic or a transition broke, I debugged it with my lecturer, online research or AI. I used AI only for code and mechanics. When a teammate fell behind, I worked out how to help them and how to keep the project moving towards the deadline. A friend sometimes programmed with me so we could take turns and breaks.

Process

The written brief didn't survive, but a dense one-page design summary did, and it's detailed enough to stand in for it. The objective is simple to state and hard to survive: find a way home, or at least survive the night, evading "the witch." Five mechanics carry that objective — exploration, escape, collection, reading, and mini-puzzles — colour-coded on the design page alongside a sixth, jumpscares, that punctuates key story beats.

The house is mapped across four levels — ground floor, second floor, basement and attic — and the story is structured as six escalating moments. It opens in the forest, where scattered objects left by the protagonist's friends nudge him deeper in as the atmosphere shifts from calm to watched. Inside the house, a note confirms Hansel and Gretel are being held somewhere below, triggering the first scare. In the basement, candlelight reveals a room of bones the moment the flame goes out — and a now-monstrous Hansel and Gretel begin the chase. Escaping locks the door behind him, but the house keeps escalating: objects move on their own, distorted voices stalk him from nearby rooms. The second floor's ruined children's bedrooms and library hide a note pointing him to a key in the attic — the game's final, darkest space, where the actual truth surfaces: Hansel and Gretel are trapped in a repeating horror cycle and need someone to take their place to be freed. The protagonist is left with one way out — a window — and one question the design leaves genuinely open: will he jump, or be caught?

Storytelling is deliberately split between explicit and implicit layers: voiceover and written notes carry the explicit narrative, while abandoned toys, blood on the walls and shadows projected without explanation carry the rest — the same "show, don't tell" logic that runs through the sound design too (creaking wood, distorted voices, sudden silence when a candle goes out).

Solution

A four-level house built specifically to support a six-beat horror structure, with escalation handled through environment rather than exposition — degrading lighting, objects that move between visits, a locking door that removes the option to backtrack, and a jumpscare reserved for the exact moment the player's skepticism should break (reading Hansel and Gretel's note in the kitchen).

Result & reflection

A finished, playable Unity game. I made the final build, and the gameplay video we submitted survives. The one-page design summary behind it is a complete game plan on its own: narrative, floor plans, a six-beat structure, mechanics, sound design and a hand-drawn storyboard.

It also led to something bigger. After I presented the game, my lecturer invited me into his research seedbed as a developer (see VR & Emotion Research).
