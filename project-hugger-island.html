Hugger Island

Market research & brand-positioning strategy · Investigación de Mercados, EAFIT · 2026

My role
Team of 5 ("Hugger Crew"). I led the buyer personas, ran interviews, built the Instagram observation tool and presented my part of the pitch to our professor
Methods
Survey, interviews, focus group, non-participant observation, buyer personas
Client (course project)
Hugger Island (educational and affective toy system), a real company brought in through an EAFIT course challenge
  • Context

    A market research course at EAFIT with a real company: Hugger Island, a plush companion with a digital ecosystem for children aged 6 to 12. Team of five.

  • Challenge

    Parents saw it as a nice emotional gift, not an educational investment. How do their economic and emotional perceptions shape that?

  • Solution

    Four research methods, three buyer personas and five sequenced strategies.

  • Result

    The barrier is categorical, not economic. Our lecturer chose the project to present to the real company, which thanked the teams that took part.

  • Learning

    Triangulating several methods shows what no single one can.

The problem

A product that parents file in the wrong box. Hugger Island's "Abrazador" combines affective bonding with cognitive and emotional development for children aged 6 to 12. But parents in economically constrained households tended to see it as a "nice-to-have emotional gift", not an educational investment, so it lost out to more visibly academic spending.

Our research question: how do parents' economic and socio-emotional perceptions shape whether they see Hugger Island as a priority educational investment? The target segment was parents aged 35 to 55 in strata 4 and 5, with children aged 6 to 12.

My role

Buyer personas, interviews and my part of the pitch, in a team of five. We worked as "Hugger Crew", met weekly and helped with each other's parts.

  • Searched for articles to ground the research and drafted questions for the survey and interviews.
  • Concentrated on the buyer personas while teammates defined the objectives and tools.
  • Ran interviews, shared the survey with our target segment, and built the observation tool for Instagram.
  • Used AI to help triangulate the results, so we could handle a lot of data without losing anything.
  • Co-wrote the final report and the strategies. I presented my pitch sections (buyer personas, digital-channel communication and insights) to our professor.

Four ways of looking

  1. Survey · 111 parents

    It mapped parents' educational spending. 95.5% rated academic performance as very important, yet socio-emotional tools had 0% of prior purchases, against 61.3% each for books and school materials.

  2. In-depth interviews

    They added the interpretive layer. One respondent with high purchase intent and full understanding of the product still called it "therapy or a gift, but never something educational."

  3. Focus group · four mothers

    They arrived without knowing the product and estimated 50,000 to 90,000 pesos. By the end, three of four called it a "necessity" and none a "luxury", and one participant's stated willingness to pay quadrupled in the same session.

  4. Observation of digital channels

    Seven sessions across the website, WhatsApp, Instagram and TikTok. None turned the brand's emotional story into visible educational outcomes, and real strengths, such as more than 102 organisations already using the system, were hidden at first contact.

The three buyer personas

Built with the Buyer Persona Institute's "5 Rings of Buying Insight". Carolina Restrepo, the primary buyer: a working mother who needs visible results (reading, focus, habits) and has no time for complexity. Andrés Rodríguez, the secondary buyer: a rational co-decision-maker who only approves spending he can justify. Paula Jiménez, a deliberate negative persona: she engages warmly with the brand's content but has no real intent to buy, included so the team wouldn't mistake her engagement for demand.

Solution

Five sequenced strategies.

  1. Reframe the narrative

    Keep the fantasy universe as the child's world of play, but tell parents what changes in their child ("learns to calm down when frustrated").

  2. Focus the product portfolio

    Stop showing the Abrazador next to T-shirts and face masks in the online store, which diluted the educational frame at the moment of purchase.

  3. Build evidence-based content

    Three layers: purpose in plain parental language, real unscripted testimonials, then validation through the business ecosystem.

  4. Show up in schools and fairs

    Use the child as an internal household "prescriber". The focus group proposed this channel spontaneously.

  5. Reframe price

    Around the cost of not investing, with familiar comparisons (a therapy session, a month of tutoring) and staged payments. Last, once the story and evidence are in place.

The report behind it

  • ~25,000words in the final report (in Spanish)
  • 14tables
  • 4specific research objectives
  • 12interviews coded, E1 to E12

The final report is a full research document. It runs from the theory and the research question, through the method, schedule, budget and validity, to the analysis of each objective and a triangulation across all four instruments. I co-wrote it, and the team analysed the results in a shared workbook, one sheet per instrument (the pictures in the gallery show them).

What is inside the report

Introduction · Epistemology · Background · Research problem and question (three categories with their axes: economic perception, pedagogical and socio-emotional value, brand positioning) · General and specific objectives · Scope and limitations · Methodology (sample, and one technique per objective) · Schedule · Budget · Validity of the research · Tool formats · Analysis of results for each objective (survey, digital channels, interviews with verbatim quotes, focus group) · Triangulation of results · Analytical synthesis for each objective, tied to the theory · References.

Result & reflection

  • 111parents surveyed
  • 78.4%called it a priority educational investment once it was described
  • 0%had ever bought a socio-emotional tool
  • 3 of 4focus-group mothers called it a necessity

The barrier is categorical, not economic. Parents already valued what the product offers (emotional regulation, autonomy, sustained attention) and linked it to school performance. They just hadn't filed Hugger Island under a budget category that let them buy it. The gap between 78.4% declared valuation and 0% prior spending was our clearest number, and the focus group showed it could close within one conversation.

Our lecturer selected the project to be presented to the real client. Two of my teammates pitched our findings and strategies to the company as input for its next steps. I couldn't attend, but I would have taken any part of it: in a group project everyone has to be ready to present every part.

The company reacted well. It rewarded the teams that worked on its brief with a small gift: a spare Abrazador, a book and an online book, a thank-you letter for each member and a notebook. Our teacher congratulated us as well.

Limits: we planned at least 150 surveys and collected 111. The focus group was small, and our report notes that its mothers were younger than the planned age range and not all in strata 4 and 5, because of the limits of fieldwork in a course.
