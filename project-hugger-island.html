Hugger Island

Market research & brand-positioning strategy · Investigación de Mercados, EAFIT · 2026

My role
Team of 5 ("Hugger Crew"). I led the buyer personas, ran interviews, did the Instagram observation and presented part of the pitch
Methods
Survey, interviews, focus group, non-participant observation, buyer personas
Client (course project)
Hugger Island (educational/affective toy system), a real company brought in through an EAFIT course challenge

The problem

Hugger Island's product — "the Abrazador," a plush companion paired with a digital ecosystem — combines affective bonding with cognitive and emotional development for children aged 6 to 12. Despite that, parents in economically constrained households (our study focused on Medellín, strata 4–5) tended to file it under "nice-to-have emotional gift" rather than "educational investment," which meant it kept losing out to more traditional, visibly academic spending. The research question: how do parents' economic and socio-emotional perceptions shape whether they see Hugger Island as a priority educational investment at all?

My role

This was a five-person team project for Investigación de Mercados at EAFIT. We worked as "Hugger Crew", met weekly and helped with each other's parts.

At the start I searched for articles to ground our research and drafted questions for the survey and interviews. As the project grew, I concentrated on the buyer personas while teammates defined the research objectives and tools. I also ran interviews, shared the survey with our target segment and built the observation tool for Instagram. With AI's help, I triangulated the results so we could make sense of a lot of data without losing anything. Finally, I co-wrote the final report and strategies, and presented the pitch sections on buyer personas, digital-channel communication and insights.

Process

We combined four instruments. A survey mapped parents' educational spending profile: 95.5% rated academic performance as very important, yet socio-emotional tools registered 0% of prior purchases, against 61.3% each for books and school materials. Interviews added the interpretive layer — one respondent with high purchase intent and full understanding of the product still called it "therapy or a gift, but never something educational." A four-mother focus group produced the sharpest evidence: entering with no knowledge of the product and estimates of 50,000–90,000 pesos, three of four reclassified it as a "necessity" (not a "luxury") by the end of a single session, and one participant's stated willingness to pay quadrupled in that same conversation. Finally, non-participant observation of five digital channels (website, WhatsApp, Instagram, TikTok) found that none translated the brand's emotional narrative into observable educational outcomes, and that real differentiators — over 102 businesses already using the system, a full digital ecosystem — were invisible at the first point of contact.

To act on those findings, we built three buyer personas using the Buyer Persona Institute's "5 Rings of Buying Insight" structure: Carolina Restrepo, the primary buyer — a working mother who needs results she can see (reading, focus, habits) and has no time for anything that adds complexity to her routine; Andrés Rodríguez, the secondary buyer — a rational co-decision-maker who only approves spending he can justify against clear, comparable benefit; and Paula Jiménez, a deliberate negative persona — someone who engages warmly with the brand's content (saves posts, follows, shares) but has no real purchase intent, included precisely so the team wouldn't mistake her engagement for demand.

Solution

We proposed five sequenced strategies. Reframe the narrative: keep the fantastical universe as the child's world of play, but speak to parents in terms of what changes in their child ("learns to calm down when frustrated") rather than the character's adventures. Focus the product portfolio: stop displaying the Abrazador alongside T-shirts and face masks in the online store, which was diluting the educational frame at the exact moment a purchase decision could close. Build evidence-based content in three layers — purpose in plain parental language, then real unscripted testimonials, then validation through the existing business ecosystem. Show up in schools and fairs, using the child as an internal household "prescriber" — a channel the focus group proposed spontaneously, not something we'd planned for. Reframe price around the cost of not investing, using familiar comparisons (a therapy session, a month of tutoring) and staged payment options — deployed last, only once the narrative and evidence are already in place.

Result & reflection

The central diagnosis: the barrier is categorical, not economic. Parents already valued the exact attributes the product offers — emotional regulation, autonomy, sustained attention — and already linked them to real school performance; they simply hadn't filed Hugger Island under a budget category that let them buy it. That gap between 78.4% declared valuation and 0% actual prior spending was the project's clearest number, and the focus group showed it could be closed within a single conversation once the full story was told.

Our lecturer selected the project to be presented to the real client. Two of the team pitched our findings and strategies to the company as input for their next steps.

Methods

  • Survey
  • Semi-structured interviews
  • Focus group
  • Non-participant observation
  • Buyer personas (5 Rings of Buying Insight)
