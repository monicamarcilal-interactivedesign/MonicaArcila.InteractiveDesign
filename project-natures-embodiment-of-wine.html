Nature's Embodiment of Wine

Themed AI image generator · Generative Graphic Design COMD 342, Victoria University of Wellington · 2026

My role
Solo — concept, prompt engineering & curation
Tools
Gemini (3.6 Extended Thinking)
Date
August 2026

The problem

The brief was to design a themed generative system — one prompt "recipe" that could produce a whole grid of images sharing a visual identity. The real problem underneath that is what Kate Compton calls the "10,000 Bowls of Oatmeal" problem: a generator can produce thousands of mathematically unique outputs that a viewer still perceives as "a lot of oatmeal" — differentiated in theory, but not distinct in practice. My process ran directly into this.

My role

Solo project: I developed the concept, wrote and diagnosed every iteration of the prompt, and curated the final grid.

Process

My concept turns animals into portraits of wine, staged in a Burlesque aesthetic — sitting at the intersection of three personal starting points: the sheer variety of animal species, wine as one of my preferred drinks, and Burlesque as the aesthetic most likely to give each animal-wine pairing a strong sense of personality. I deliberately moved the tone away from an early, more flirtatious framing and toward something gentler — closer to imagining a pet in a costume than anything seductive.

About fifteen prompt iterations in, my instinct was that being highly specific — exact expressions, exact framing, exact accessories — would give the model more to work with and therefore produce more variety. The opposite happened: over-specifying the character produced images that repeated the same background, the same pose, and the same attempt to spell the wine's name onto the animal, regardless of which animal or wine I asked for.

The fix wasn't more detail — it was a change in what the detail controlled. Instead of prescribing a fixed expression or scene, I isolated each recurring failure point one at a time (background repetition, pose repetition, literal text appearing in the image) and added a narrow, targeted constraint against just that failure, while leaving everything else — the animal's specific personality, its exact posture — open for the model to interpret. That shift, from prescribing the outcome to constraining the failure modes, is what let each animal read as a distinct character instead of a re-used template.

The same logic shaped the final prompt's anatomy constraint: "the animal's natural anatomy must not be changed more than 80%." I wanted the animals to stay recognisable, real creatures rather than fully invented mascots, so the constraint gives the model a defined, non-zero margin (roughly 20%) to interpret personality through physical detail — instead of leaving "realism" as an unmeasurable, subjective instruction. Framing it as a percentage made it something I could actually check the output against.

One specific, testable example: an earlier version of the prompt was missing the line "avoid at all costs including letters or words in the animal" — and, tested on a tortoise embodying a bottle of Port, produced a shell lettered with "VINTAGE PORT" and "BURLESQUE." Adding that single explicit constraint fixed it: the same tortoise, same props (a vest, a chair, a gramophone), but with personality carried entirely through pose and staging instead of text.

Solution

The final recipe: "…generate a quite unrealistic image of an [animal] embodying and expressing in their character a bottle of [wine] wine… soft diffused lighting, velvet drapery, warm gold and deep red tones, vintage cabaret staging… the animal's natural anatomy must not be changed more than 80%, it must be recognisable, so no forced facial expressions or human features… avoid at all costs including letters or words… personality is expressed through pose, props and attitude, not through altering the animal's face." Two variables — animal and wine — run through one fixed staging system.

Result & reflection

I consider the final grid a strong resolution of the brief I set for myself: each animal-wine pairing is recognisable as the same animal, staged in one coherent Burlesque system, while still carrying its own distinct idea — a porcupine reclining like royalty, a cobra in a top hat, a jaguar draped over velvet, a chameleon holding a cocktail glass — rather than blending into the rest of the set.

The clearest lesson was that fixing a generative-design problem like "10,000 Bowls of Oatmeal" isn't about adding more instructions — it's about finding exactly which failure mode is repeating and constraining only that, one at a time, and checking each fix against a visible before/after result.
