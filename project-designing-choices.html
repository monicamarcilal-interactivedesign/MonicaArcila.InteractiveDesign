What Kind of Animal Would You Be During a Life Crisis?

Branching interactive quiz · DSDN142, Victoria University of Wellington · 2026

My role
Solo — concept, UX writing, interaction design, visual system and AI-assisted build
Tools
Figma Design, Figma Make, Claude, Gemini
Timeframe
13 August – 3 September 2026

The problem

The brief asked for a decision-driven interactive web experience with a landing page, at least five choices the user must interact with, and at least three distinct endings — built in Figma and made functional with Figma Make. Because it's an interaction design brief and not just a visual one, looking good wasn't enough: every choice had to genuinely change the tone and outcome, and the whole thing had to stay intuitive enough for someone who had never seen it before to click through confidently.

The hardest part was holding two things at once: real interaction design (choices that meaningfully branch, not just look different) and a style simple enough to build with my own skills in the time available.

My role

This was a solo project. I wrote the branching narrative for both paths, designed the interaction model (affordances, feedback, navigation), built the visual system (colour, type, a small reactive character), and used Claude and Gemini throughout — not to generate the design for me, but to test branching logic, draft and revise choice text, and translate what I wanted structurally into the exact language Figma Make needs (variables and conditional actions).

Process

My concept is a two-path personality quiz: at the start, the user picks which "crisis" to live through — a Millennial path (the slow realisation that divorce is an option at 40) or a Gen Z path (a workplace-and-dating crisis). Both paths share the same underlying mechanism — five escalating choices, three pattern-based endings — but each has its own scenario, tone and set of animal archetypes, so the format itself comments on generational difference rather than just swapping the setting.

  1. 13 August — Pure confusion

    Started from a Figma flowchart brief-check with Claude. My first low-fidelity wireframe only had "start → decision" nodes with no visible connections, and duplicate screens instead of one clean path. I chose to build the Millennial path properly before touching Gen Z, and ideated the starter situations and options with Claude until the concept felt right.

  2. 28 August — Style and desktop design

    With all five Millennial choices written as a breakup narrative, I moved to how the quiz looked. I built a very basic reactive character with Gemini to carry facial expressions for each choice, and chose a five-colour palette — a near-black background with fully saturated neon accents (pink, cyan, lime) — deliberately pushed past "clean and modern" toward something closer to a warning label, so the visual tone would escalate the same way the choice text does.

  3. 29 August — Structural problems

    A rubric re-check caught that choices 1–4 weren't actually affecting which ending a user got — only choice 5 was. I fixed this by tagging every option across all five choices to one of three archetypes and switching to a tally-based system, which also meant dropping a fourth possible ending to keep the pattern legible.

  4. 1 September — Building the second path

    Once the Millennial path's tagging system worked, I mirrored the structure into the Gen Z path with an entirely different scenario, grounding the situations in real generational research (a BBC article on how today's 20-somethings have it worse, plus current internet culture) and landing on a distinct set of archetypes: Cat, Possum and Capybara.

  5. 3 September — Feedback mechanism

    Finished the Gen Z endings and built the feedback layer in Figma Make: a reactive "echo" line that responds to the specific option just picked, so the interface always acknowledges the user's last action even without a visible running score.

Solution

The final experience is a two-path branching quiz, each path built from five escalating choices that tally toward one of three animal archetypes. A few interaction decisions carry most of the design intent:

Locked choices. Once an option is picked, there's no going back — simpler to build reliably in Figma Make, and it reinforces the emotional weight of each choice, the way you don't get to undo the decision in the situation being lived, either.

Visible affordances. Buttons have a clear rest state (a thin border) and hover state (a solid fill, the box widens slightly), following Norman's principle of visible signifiers — interactivity reads before the click.

A reactive character. On each choice, everything blurs except an avatar and a reaction bubble matching the option picked; that same line follows the user onto the next screen as a plain italic reminder, so a choice's emotional consequence stays visible for one extra screen instead of disappearing the moment you move on.

Result & reflection

A fully functional, two-path branching experience in Figma Make that meets every requirement of the brief: a landing page, five choices per path, and three distinct endings that are actually earned by the path taken rather than decided by the last click.

The biggest thing I learned was to use AI as a translation layer, not a content generator: Claude and Gemini were most useful for turning a structural idea already in my head into the exact variable-and-conditional-logic language Figma Make needed, and for pressure-testing whether a branching pattern actually held together before I built it.
