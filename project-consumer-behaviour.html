Consumer Behaviour: a profile and a brand strategy

Consumer research & marketing strategy · MARK 202 Consumer Behaviour, Victoria University of Wellington · 2026

My role
Individual project: research, analysis, strategy and written report
Methods
Seven structured written questionnaires, secondary research, course theory
Output
A 35-page report and a five-minute oral discussion
Timeframe
Trimester 2, 2026
  • Context

    MARK 202 Consumer Behaviour at Victoria University of Wellington, on exchange. An individual project: profile one real consumer, then market a new footwear brand to her.

  • Challenge

    Understand one buyer well enough to justify every marketing decision (product, price, place and promotion) with her own words and with theory.

  • Solution

    Seven written questionnaires across her decision journey, perception, learning, personality and social world, then a profile and 12 specific recommendations for "Ryegraz", a retro-runner brand.

  • Result

    A 35-page report that traces each recommendation back to a finding. One surprising finding: she is loyal to a brand that does not reflect who she is.

  • Learning

    A recommendation is only as strong as the evidence behind it. Every one needs the consumer's own words.

The problem

Know the person before you design for her. The brief: choose a consumer who recently made a major footwear purchase after a slow, rational decision, and build a profile useful to a marketing manager. Then use it to market a new shoe brand and justify each choice, in writing and in a five-minute oral discussion.

Every recommendation had to be specific to her, not to "shoe buyers" in general.

My role

Researcher, analyst and strategist: the whole project was individual.

  • Chose the consumer: a 20-year-old international exchange student studying design, who had just bought FILA sneakers.
  • Ran seven structured written questionnaires with her, plus secondary research.
  • Analysed the answers with the course's concepts and wrote the profile and the recommendations.
  • Prepared the oral justification. I used AI to help synthesise my findings from the tutorials into the marketing mix, then rewrote it by hand in my own words to check I understood every recommendation. The oral discussion went well.
  • Used AI to help organise the written data, edit my first sketches and format the report. The consumer data, answers and analytical judgements are my own, and the report says so.

Six lenses on one buyer

  1. Who she is

    Segmented by usage and situation more than by age or income. The tension that drives her: durability for Wellington's hills and weather against an uncompromising personal style. Her Google ad settings added a little secondary data.

  2. How she decides

    A long, comparative search. Asked to split 100 points, she gave 40 to visual design, 40 to functionality and 20 to comfort, and said she buys only when all three score 100. That is a non-compensatory rule: a strength in one cannot make up for a weakness in another.

  3. How she perceives

    She reads brands through visual and structural cues, not written claims. A visible cutaway of a midsole was both a style statement and a promise of performance.

  4. How she learns

    Familiarity came from simply seeing the brand often. Her wish to repurchase comes from proven quality, and one bad experience taught her to avoid a whole category of brands.

  5. Who she is, and who she is not

    Big Five scores, plus a brand-personality comparison. She disagreed that the brand reflects who she is, and prefers brands that are not mainstream.

  6. Who influences her

    Three reference groups. Family has little say, friends give information she weighs, and an aspirational retro-fashion community shapes her taste the most.

What stood out

  • Finding

    Loyal without identity

    She plans to buy the same FILA shoes again, yet disagrees that the brand is "consistent with how I see myself". Her loyalty comes from proven performance, not from self-image.

  • Finding

    Proof beats claims

    A visible sign of how the shoe is built, such as a cutaway, is read as evidence. It is harder to dismiss than a slogan.

  • Finding

    Careful in person, quick online

    She researches a lot when shopping in a shop, but often buys online with little research. That matters for where the brand should sell.

The recommendations

Twelve recommendations for Ryegraz, each tied to a finding.

  • Product

    Pass both tests at once

    Do not trade design against performance. Use natural materials (suede, recycled canvas) with a visibly shown construction, an understated tonal logo, and spare laces and a cleaning cloth in the box.

  • Price

    NZ$259, with no luxury cues

    Below her stated ceiling of NZ$400 to limit the sense of risk, and without packaging or language that reads as a "high brand", which she rejects.

  • Place

    Shops first, calm ones

    Physical retail as the main channel, with natural light, few customers at a time and self-service browsing, so she can take her time without being approached.

  • Promotion

    Be found, then be tried

    Invest in search channels rather than interrupting her, run a two-month sampling-led launch, and avoid mass-market or influencer messaging that would make the brand feel mainstream.

Result & reflection

  • 35pages with appendices
  • ~2,900words in the main report
  • 7structured questionnaires
  • 12recommendations, each with its evidence

A profile that explains the "why" behind each decision. The report closes with a short persona summary and recommendations that each name the finding that justifies them.

The limits: the consumer agreed to take part on the condition that I share no personal data about her, so she is not named here. All the data was collected in writing, at the consumer's request, not through spoken interviews, and one consumer is treated as representative of a wider segment.

Why it belongs in a design portfolio: it is the research half of design. It is a full understanding of a person and a brand, and of how each decision follows from evidence.
